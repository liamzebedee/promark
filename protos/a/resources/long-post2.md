## Housing crisis.

Today I wrote a long letter to my local member about the housing crisis. I focused on describing how I imagine productive growth. There was a lot there.

> The point of a city is economic growth. Cities are born from opportunity - think of gold rushes. Cities die when opportunity dries up. 
> 
> Cities exist because proximity reduces the cost of exchanging  goods, services, and ideas. Dense clusters of people and firms share  infrastructure, specialize more, and innovate faster because information flows more easily.
>
> More people in cities  strengthens these network effects: more skills, more businesses, more  cultural variety, more ideas colliding. That raises productivity and  wealth per person. Housing limits block this by forcing people out,  raising costs, and weakening the very reason cities form. So more  housing supply preserves and amplifies the productivity engine that  makes cities valuable.

Another aspect I touched on was *productivity*:

> Another aspect to this is productivity
>
> Take the government's decision to give out taxpayer money so people can buy homes (homebuyer grants). Is it bad? In a way this is not too dissimilar to in 2007 when the government gave all kids (students) free laptops. This I find interesting. 
>
> On one hand, houses are productive. You cannot work very effectively if you are homeless, this is obvious. Once you own a house it gives you a foundation to be productive. 
> 
> On the other hand, that productivity has a ceiling. You can't make a lot of businesses from just owning a house. However if you have a laptop, you realistically have a huge number of productive activities you can do. You can write and sell software, you can setup an online business in many forms - ecommerce, social media marketing, developing a new DeFi protocol, videography, the list goes on...
> 
> Still thinking about it all

I realised something fundamental about **cities**. With cities, you pay for access to the network. That is primarily the cost. If you value Sydney, Brisbane, and Melbourne, they are all marginally the same for me. Compared to SF, Sydney's network is maybe 100-1000x less valuable.

## Romanticism, and the call to adventure.

I miss longform reading and writing. I recently picked up a [piece by Scott Farquar](https://www.startupdaily.net/advice/opinion/tech-council-boss-scott-farquhar-on-australias-ai-future-and-techno-optimism-to-team-up-with-government/) where he talked about the next industrial revolution which is AI. It was a scintillating piece, well-written, with new ideas. 

He begins: 

> To understand the scale of the AI opportunity, it helps to look back. The history of human progress is also the history of how we have harnessed energy in new ways to do useful work.
>
> Historically, these activities were physical.  It was once _hard_ to move people across a continent. It was once _hard_ to cross an ocean.  It was once _hard_ to plough a field.
>
> Now we barely think about them. Because what was once hard becomes easy.  I’ll say that again.  When we can harness energy in new ways, what was once hard becomes easy.

A great metaphor and way of storytelling, rule of 3's. The ideas he mentioned don't even come into my purview now, but upon reading it, it makes instant sense. 

> And when that happens, nations can rise, or fall, based on how they respond.  As someone who has spent most of my life at the cutting edge of technology, I know that when new technologies are created small initial advantages can compound over time into unassailable leads.
>
> 200 years ago England benefited from accessible coal, which led to railways, which made coal even more accessible.  That early lead led them to invent the Watts steam engine and the Bessemer process for creating steel. On the back of coal powered steam and steel they built a navy—and an empire that lasted a century.
>
> Today, we’re at another inflection point. We’re entering the next energy revolution, but this time using energy to power knowledge work.  Just as we have gotten comfortable using energy to power physical work, we need to get comfortable using energy to power knowledge work.

It is so prescient, considering Australia's position as a high-quality knowledge economy backed on a _third-world resource export base_. And with that, I found it quite interesting how he drew our attention towards something blending both that physical and cognitive:

> We have abundant natural advantages: energy, vast land, a strong legal system, world-class engineers, and a strategic location in Southeast Asia.  Just like Singapore, we can press those advantages.
> 
> This brings me to the first opportunity I want to talk about today.  Australia has the opportunity to host data centres for the entire Southeast Asia region and potentially beyond.
> 
> ...
>
> When Netflix or Amazon or Google or Facebook choose where to put a data centre, they consider a lot of factors: how much it costs to build, the price and availability of power and water, the laws of the country, the talent density to build and run it and finally, in this world of tariffs and geopolitics the availability of cutting edge computer chips.
> 
> On all of these factors, Australia scores well.  We are surprisingly cost competitive building data centres.  We have the talent density and attractively priced green power.  But there are areas which hold us back from an even bigger opportunity.   We should power the region.  We should export megawatts as megabytes (and get paid megabucks). This is a multi billion dollar, multi decade opportunity.  To do that, we need to systematically remove barriers to growth.

Fascinating idea.

### A bit of poetry.

The sensation I would like to end on. 

> one thing I wish was more present in australian media is the romantic idealism of the outback. we used to have bush poets

The idea of reading and writing in the longest form has been lost in this day and age. I constantly swing back to reflecting on this, even losing the habit within myself, finding it hard to finish an entire novel because it just doesn't meet my thresholds for dopamine (or something along those lines). But reading that Scott Farquar piece really put me in a mood. 

I realise that diminished attention span doesn't just affect substance, it affects ambition. After joining the Build Australia group and then promptly missing their first meetup, I really realised in their "summary 1st plan" how bored I was by it. Nothing they proposed truly excites me, it is very lame and small-minded. I wonder if this quality is really just my latent ambitiousness, that I've failed to notice.

Personally I would love to erupt back into the blogging era, and today I am coming to realise what that means. It means longform reading and longform writing, where the content is in its purest form, unoptimized for an algorithm. I wonder how we get back there, and I think it comes in the form of a bloggers network once again. Substack is all too easy, but I find myself never drawn to it, as it's fraught with centralization danger.

I hope that this blog is one step into it. A daily reflection, in long-form, inside headings, in a format/style I control, with quotes, and potential for more. One cool idea is LLM-driven summaries on the main page. This is maybe an incantation of something greater to come - after all, what is an algorithm but a persuasion device? Maybe LLM's can be our dripfeed into something more textual and less...brainrotting.
